**Page 1 — Introduction**

Good morning. We are Safari. Our project studies attention allocation and navigation information design for newcomers to Hong Kong. Today we will explain the detailed plan, how we will document navigation episodes, and what still needs checking before collecting data.

---

**Page 2 — Research question**

Our research question is: How can navigation information be classified by its associated interaction burdens to inform interaction design methods for improving when and how it is presented and delivered?

We will examine what people need to confirm at particular moments, then connect those needs with their actions and reported experience.

---

**Page 3 — Knowledge gap and literature review**

Before diving into our plan, let's have a look at our knowledge gap, which is: in unfamiliar walking navigation, which information episodes and interactions add attention and interaction burden, which effectively support orientation, action and confirmation, and how do these differences vary with context?

The eight studies provide complementary evidence. Vaez shows that digital navigation changes strategies without necessarily reducing anxiety. Wickens explains competition for shared processing resources, and Oulasvirta shows fragmented attention in urban mobile tasks. Cheng examines the trade-off between map information, spatial learning and cognitive load in VR. Golab studies when pedestrians request instructions, while Pielot examines tactile navigation in everyday use. Shimokihara reports associations between phone glances, stops and errors but leaves their temporal order unresolved. Hardwicke shows how wayfinding information helps users judge manageable walks.

Together, these findings motivate studying specific information interactions in context. We will connect actions and participant explanations to distinguish experienced burden from useful guidance, confirmation and reassurance. Our methods do not directly measure the amount of attention consumed or cognitive load. The next slide illustrates why successful arrival can still involve an effortful experience.

---

**Page 4 — Repulse Bay example**

Imagine a newcomer planning a relaxing day at Repulse Bay. They first find the right exit, follow street turns, resolve unclear cues and confirm the beach entrance.

The chart illustrates two possible experiences, not collected data. Clear, timely cues may keep the journey manageable. In the more demanding scenario, the traveller repeatedly switches between the map, signs and streets to work out what to do. They still reach the beach, but feel too drained to enjoy it.

Both scenarios reach the destination. The difference is the effort experienced along the way. This motivates our question: which information episodes and interactions support navigation, and which add attention or interaction burden? Our actual study examines a short unfamiliar walk, rather than testing a whole Repulse Bay trip or measuring energy depletion.

---

**Page 5 — Paradigm, theoretical lens and method**

Pragmatism guides our method choices because we want evidence that can inform design decisions in this context. Multiple Resource Theory is a sensitising lens for considering competing demands. We will use a focused qualitative study, with one complete walk as one case. Observation and later interviews let us examine actions and participants' explanations together.

---

**Page 6 — Why these methods?**

Each method helps us understand a different part of the same journey. Observation records the actions and their surroundings. Semi-structured interviews explore what participants were trying to do and how the experience felt. Reviewing selected episodes connects those explanations to specific moments. We then analyse each walk and compare cases to develop contextual information categories and preliminary design guidance.

---

**Page 7 — Research objectives**

We have four research objectives.

First, we will identify information needs and observable interaction episodes across an unfamiliar walking journey.

Second, we will identify participant-reported moments of feeling overwhelmed, examine possible sources of difficulty, and explore perceived consequences for enjoyment, confidence, continued exploration and reaching the destination.

Third, we will develop a classification linking information roles, contextual demands and participant explanations, including contrasting cases.

Finally, we will derive evidence-linked guidance for presenting timing, detail and delivery, with clear limits on what has been evaluated.

**Page 9 — Participants and recruitment**

We aim to recruit six main participants: adults who arrived in Hong Kong within the past six months and are unfamiliar with the selected route. Recruitment and completed sessions remain unconfirmed. Everyone will use Google Maps, while we record previous app experience and language settings. Each participant walks individually, without peers coaching them.

---

**Page 10 — Why start with six participants?**

We acknowledge the limits of this study. Given the project timeline, this is a focused, exploratory study in which each case combines a walking observation with an in-depth interview. We propose six participants as an initial target for three reasons.

First, we will screen participants against our study criteria, so each case is relevant to the research question. Screening supports relevance; it does not guarantee rich data.

Second, six cases are feasible within the schedule while allowing us to examine each journey and interview in depth, then compare cases.

Third, we will review the evidence iteratively. If key questions remain unresolved, we will recruit further participants to address those gaps. We will assess whether interpretations are well supported and whether additional cases bring substantively new insights, rather than assume that six guarantees saturation. If time prevents further recruitment, we will narrow our claims and report the remaining limitations.
