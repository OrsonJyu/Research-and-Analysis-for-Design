## Slide 01 · Recap — 30 s · 0:00–0:30

Good morning. We are Safari. Today we report our data collection progress on how newcomers to Hong Kong use navigation information.

Our question is: **how can navigation information be classified by its interaction burdens, to improve when and how it is delivered?**

We answer it through field observation, interviews, and analysis that links the two. In ten minutes we cover three parts: how we set up the fieldwork, what our data look like after filtering, and what comes next.

## Slide 02 · Route candidates — 25 s · 0:30–0:55

We first compared three destinations from PolyU South Gate: K11 Art Mall, Kwun Yum Temple and the Clock Tower. The times shown are planning estimates, not measured walks.

**We chose K11 and extended it**: Art Mall became a required waypoint, and the task ends at the Barbour store inside K11 MUSEA. Two reasons follow.

## Slide 03 · Participant familiarity — 25 s · 0:55–1:20

The first reason is the participants. **None of our five participants knew this route**, and none had visited the Barbour store.

This lets us watch how people seek information in an unfamiliar place, instead of following a route from memory. Their navigation habits still differ, and we keep that in mind when we interpret behaviour.

## Slide 04 · Environmental conditions — 35 s · 1:20–1:55

The second reason is the environment. The route runs from PolyU, through Art Mall, to the third floor of K11 MUSEA.

On the way, participants meet **dense commercial streets, wide roads, narrow lanes and many junctions**, plus landmark buildings. At the end, they must **find a floor and a shop indoors**.

These were our reasons for choosing the route; which features each person actually met comes from their own records.

## Slide 05 · Warm-up — 25 s · 1:55–2:20

Before the formal task, each participant does a short campus warm-up, from the library to the Innovation Tower. They navigate as usual; switching apps or asking people is fine.

**The warm-up lets participant and researcher practise the recording, not the route**, and its records stay out of the main analysis.

## Slide 06 · Fieldwork workflow — 45 s · 2:20–3:05

Here is our fieldwork workflow.

During the walk, **the participant wears a microphone**, so we capture what they say naturally. **The researcher films the walk and records voice memos** of what they see, because writing while walking is hard.

Afterwards, we transcribe the voice memos and check them into observation logs. We then use those logs and selected video clips in a **semi-structured interview**, where participants explain what they were checking and why.

Keep this chain in mind: memo, video, interview. It is exactly how we filter the data later.

## Slide 07 · Data collection progress — 30 s · 3:05–3:35

Where are we now? **All five navigation observations are complete; two interviews remain.**

Our material has four types: video for context and verification, observation logs from the memos, GPS tracks for route context, and interview records for explanations.

So today we present organised data and early patterns, **not final findings**.

## Slide 08 · Action tags — 45 s · 3:35–4:20

To organise the logs we use descriptive action tags.

S is a stop. P is looking at the phone; I is touching or typing on it. H is a head turn, and G is looking at an identifiable sign. Q is a help request we can hear or reliably document. R is a researcher prompt.

Fieldwork added two codes, now **unified across all five cases**: **T, talking with the researcher**, and **K, using a public kiosk** such as a mall directory.

These tags describe what is visible. **They are not yet our qualitative codebook.**

## Slide 09 · Data filtering — 50 s · 4:20–5:10

This is how we filtered the data.

**We start from the voice-memo observation logs.** Each logged moment is then **checked against the video**, where we confirm, correct or add details. Where we have an interview, we use the participant's account to **corroborate** the moment. Finally, we **re-describe each selected segment as a code combination**, for example stop-and-phone, then stop-and-head-turn, together with its location, situation and environment.

On the right: about thirty-four to thirty-six segments per walk; Peter's seventy records use an earlier, ungraded format. Stars mark key segments. **Grades show what we will code first, not how hard a moment was.**

## Slide 10 · Data case — 55 s · 5:10–6:05

Here is one filtered segment, from Tom.

Google Maps re-routed him into a back service lane: narrow, cluttered, no street names. The codes show the sequence. He follows a passer-by in, checks his screen while turning, keeps checking while watching workers, and then **stops and zooms the map with both hands for about eight seconds**.

The voice memo said he was unsure and kept checking the map; **the video confirms that**. In the interview, Tom called this lane **the most stressful part of the walk**, "not like a proper road".

**Observation, cross-check and his own account stay in separate fields.** Our interpretation is added only last.

## Slide 11 · Voice memos checked against video — 50 s · 6:05–6:55

Checking every memo against video changed our record in three ways.

**Confirmed**: many memo notes hold up, such as Tom's continuous phone checking on a straight road.

**Corrected**: at a crowded crossing, the memo said Tom used his phone more. The video shows the opposite, **his lowest phone viewing of the walk**; his attention was on the crowd and the lights. Another memo placed Chenggong at the Art Mall entrance, but his screen shows K11 MUSEA.

**Added**: details the memo missed, such as Tom alternating between a tunnel sign and his phone three times.

The lesson: **holding a phone is not looking at it**, so P is coded only from visible gaze.

## Slide 12 · Possible findings so far — 70 s · 6:55–8:05

Across the five walks, five candidate patterns are emerging. A filled dot means the pattern appears in that person's filtered segments.

One: **at the Art Mall waypoint, the hand-over to the next leg is unclear**, in four of five walks.

Two: **underground crossings need extra checking**; the map gives little sign that a tunnel is the way across.

Three: **seeing the target building does not show the way in**. Tom, for example, reached a hotel driveway instead of the mall entrance.

Four: **indoors, everyone turned to extra sources**: kiosks, directories, shop signs or another app. This is the only pattern seen in all five.

Five: **the phone is often held low while walking**, with checks clustering at junctions and stops.

An empty dot means not yet found, not absent. **These are candidates to test, not findings.**

## Slide 13 · Data processing — 35 s · 8:05–8:40

Next, these segments become information-interaction events.

Observation logs are the main source; video verifies actions and timing; GPS adds location.

Each event keeps **three layers apart**: what was observed, what the participant said, and what we interpret. **An event record links context, information source, action sequence and the participant's account**, so every later claim can be traced back to evidence.

## Slide 14 · Challenges and adjustments — 35 s · 8:40–9:15

Four challenges shaped our method.

Writing while walking is hard, so we use voice memos. Transcripts need manual checking, with video for verification. Kiosks and researcher talk added actions, so **we unified them as T and K and are back-checking earlier records**.

And most importantly, **behaviour does not explain itself**: a stop or a phone glance alone does not prove someone is lost or overloaded.

## Slide 15 · Next steps — 30 s · 9:15–9:45

Next, we will **finish the two remaining interviews** and align them with the observation logs. Then we check whether the evidence is enough.

If yes, we build the qualitative codebook and test the five candidate patterns within and across cases. If not, we recruit one more participant and repeat the workflow.

**Our aim is a clear line from what happened, to what participants said, to what we can claim.** Thank you.
